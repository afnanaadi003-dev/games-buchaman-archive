James Buchanan served as the 15th U.S. President during the perilous years preceding the American Civil War. A veteran diplomat, Secretary of State, and Senator from Pennsylvania, his presidency was defined by the Dred Scott decision, territorial conflict in Kansas, financial panic, and his strict constitutional view on the legalities of Southern secession.

Historical Treatise

The Buchanan Presidency & The Gathering Storm (1857–1861)

Comprehensive breakdown of antebellum diplomacy, territorial strife, economic disruption, and executive secession doctrine

I. Antebellum Politics & Pre-Presidency Career

James Buchanan brought over three decades of high-level government service to the presidency in March 1857. Having served as a U.S. Representative, Senator from Pennsylvania, Secretary of State under James K. Polk (supervising the Oregon Treaty and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo), and Minister to Great Britain under Franklin Pierce, Buchanan was widely considered one of the most qualified statesmen of his era.

While serving as Minister to the Court of St. James's, he co-authored the controversial 1854 Ostend Manifesto, which suggested that the United States should acquire Cuba from Spain, by purchase or force if necessary. This document alienated Northern anti-slavery advocates but solidified his standing among Southern Democrats, securing him the Democratic presidential nomination in 1856 as a unifying national candidate who was untainted by the bloody Kansas-Nebraska Act debates.

II. The Dred Scott Decision & Kansas Lecompton Constitution

Two days after Buchanan's inauguration, Chief Justice Roger B. Taney delivered the landmark Dred Scott v. Sandford decision. In his inaugural address, Buchanan had hinted at the upcoming ruling, urging all citizens to abide by the Supreme Court's constitutional determination. The decision declared that African Americans were not citizens and that Congress lacked constitutional power to prohibit slavery in federal territories, rendering the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.

Instead of quelling sectional turmoil, the ruling enraged Northern Republicans and anti-slavery Democrats. Buchanan further inflamed the territorial controversy in "Bleeding Kansas" by backing the pro-slavery Lecompton Constitution in 1857–1858, despite its rejection by the majority of Kansas voters. This decision split the Democratic Party, putting Buchanan into direct conflict with Senator Stephen A. Douglas.

III. The Panic of 1857 & Diplomatic Milestones

Late in 1857, the American economy was struck by the Panic of 1857, triggered by the collapse of the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company, railroad over-expansion, and falling grain prices. Buchanan responded with strict Democratic fiscal conservatism, opposing federal relief intervention, advocating specie-backed paper currency, and urging states to reform banking laws.

In international affairs, the administration achieved notable highlights. In August 1858, Buchanan exchanged the first transatlantic telegraph cable messages with Queen Victoria. In 1859, he dispatched General Winfield Scott to peacefully arbitrate the Pig War border standoff with Britain in the San Juan Islands. In May 1860, Buchanan welcomed the first official Japanese Embassy delegation to Washington, establishing formal diplomatic and commercial ties with East Asia.

IV. The Election of 1860, Secession Crisis & Executive Inaction Debate

Following the November 1860 election of Republican Abraham Lincoln, South Carolina passed its ordinance of secession on December 20, 1860, followed shortly by six other Deep South states. In his final Annual Message to Congress on December 3, 1860, Buchanan articulated his dual constitutional doctrine: he argued that while individual state secession was illegal and unconstitutional, the federal government possessed no explicit constitutional power to use military force to keep a state in the Union against its will.

Attempting to prevent armed conflict before Lincoln took office, Buchanan supported peace efforts such as the failed Crittenden Compromise and the Washington Peace Conference of 1861. While he refused to surrender federal forts in the South, including Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor, his attempt to resupply the fort via the civilian steamship Star of the West in January 1861 turned back after coming under fire. Buchanan handed over the executive office to Lincoln on March 4, 1861, leaving the Union on the brink of civil war.

The Constitutional Secession Paradox

The four pillars of Buchanan's antebellum executive doctrine during the 1860–1861 crisis

1. Illegal Secession

Maintained that state secession was an unconstitutional act of revolution without legal justification under the U.S. Constitution.

2. Coercion Limits

Asserted that the Federal Executive possessed no explicit constitutional mandate or power to wage war against a seceding state.

3. Defense of Forts

Refused to surrender federal military installations in Charleston Harbor, maintaining U.S. property claims at Fort Sumter.

4. Peace Conciliation

Encouraged congressional compromise measures like the Crittenden Compromise to avert armed conflict prior to Lincoln's inauguration.

White House Hostess & Patron

Harriet Lane: The "Democratic Queen"

As James Buchanan was America's only lifelong bachelor president, his orphaned niece Harriet Lane served as official White House Hostess. Highly educated and socially adept, "Hal" Lane won widespread acclaim for her diplomatic hosting, handling seating arrangements among rival sectional leaders with exceptional tact, promoting American art, and advocating for Native American welfare.

Diplomatic Excellence

Acclaimed host for the 1860 reception of Prince Edward (future King Edward VII).

Philanthropic Legacy

Founded the Harriet Lane Home for Invalid Children at Johns Hopkins Hospital.
